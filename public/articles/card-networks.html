Payments, explained by one receipt

Follow ฿1,000 through Visa

อ่านภาษาไทย

You tap a KBank credit card at a bike shop in Chiang Mai. The terminal says Approved in under two seconds. Here is everything that happened, the four jobs the card network did, and why the shop next door only takes PromptPay.

Start with what Visa and Mastercard are not, because most people guess wrong. They did not issue your card. That was Kasikornbank. They did not sign up the bike shop or hand it a terminal. That was an acquiring bank such as SCB, or a payment company such as 2C2P or Omise. They do not print cards, build hardware, or lend you the money.

What they run is a network. Two networks, really: a telecom network that carries messages, and a banking network that moves money. On top of both sit a fee schedule and a rulebook. That is the whole company. To see why each piece matters, follow one payment from tap to receipt to bank statement.

The bike shop cannot ask KBank whether your card is good. It has no relationship with KBank. It sees cards from every Thai bank and, in Chiang Mai, from banks in Japan, China, and Germany. The card network is the one counterparty everybody has, so it becomes the one path everything takes.

PromptPay (พร้อมเพย์) looks like it skips the middle, but it does not. It is a different network, the domestic switch run by National ITMX under the Bank of Thailand, with a different fee model. Keep that in mind for stage 3.

STAGE 1 · 0 to 2 secondsAuthorize

The terminal reads your card number, the PAN. The first six to eight digits are the BIN, the bank identification number. Think of the PAN as an IP address and the BIN as its routing prefix. The network looks at the BIN and knows exactly which issuer to forward the message to. It does not need to know anything else.

Seven hops, and at the end nothing has moved but a promise. Now the same ฿1,000 at the shop next door, which only takes PromptPay.

Five hops, and the money is gone by the second one. The card flow asks a question, may I?, and money follows a day later. The PromptPay flow issues an instruction, send, and the switch only needs to know which bank owns the ID. That is why it is faster, why it is cheaper, and why there is nothing to reverse. Keep both pictures in mind; stages 2 through 4 are the card network doing the work PromptPay skips.

Mastercard calls this job switching, and that is the right mental model. The network is a very expensive, very careful packet switch. Visa runs its own data centers and leases its own fiber. Its main US facility is built to survive earthquakes and 170 mile-per-hour winds, sits behind hydraulic bollards, and has an honest-to-goodness moat.

Thailand has its own switch too. The Bank of Thailand requires domestic debit card transactions to be switched inside the country, which is why Thai debit cards carry the TPN mark, the Thai Payment Network, alongside or instead of Visa and Mastercard. Your credit card transaction still rides Visa.

The approval is not a payment yet. It is a hold. KBank has set aside ฿1,000 of your credit line and promised to pay. The bike shop has not received anything. At the end of the day the shop sends a second message called clearing, with the final amount. Clearing is what turns a promise into a debt.

STAGE 2 · overnightSettle

Now money has to move. KBank owes the bike shop's acquirer ฿1,000. But KBank also owes every other acquirer for every other KBank card used today, and every other issuer owes the acquirer for every other card the shop accepted. Millions of tiny debts, all crossing.

The network's second job is to be the counterparty in the middle. It has a settlement account relationship with every issuer and every acquirer, so it can collect from one and pay another. And it does not settle each transaction. It nets them.

Inside Thailand this is fairly routine. Visa settles domestic transactions in baht, and the banks square up through BAHTNET, the Bank of Thailand's interbank system. The interesting case is the Japanese tourist at the next table. Her issuer is in Tokyo and pays in yen. The bike shop's acquirer is in Bangkok and wants baht. Visa holds banking relationships in both countries, converts the currency, and each bank only ever deals with Visa's local bank. The network is an adapter between national banking systems. Without it, every Thai acquirer would need its own correspondent bank in every country its tourists come from.

Being the counterparty is also where the real risk lives. If a bank fails to pay in, Visa still has to pay out. From Visa's 2024 annual report, in US dollars because the exposure is global:

STAGE 3 · the feeSplit

The bike shop does not get ฿1,000. On a typical Thai credit card sale it gets around ฿975. The missing ฿25 is the merchant discount rate, and where it goes explains almost everything about why cards work the way they do, and why so many Thai shops would rather you scan a QR code.

Read that again. The company whose name is on the card keeps a baht and a half. The bank that issued it keeps twenty. The network sets both of those numbers, and it deliberately pays itself the least.

The stated reason is risk. If your card is stolen, zero-liability rules mean KBank eats the loss, not you and not the shop. If you never pay your bill, KBank still paid the shop. The twenty baht is the price of that guarantee. Merchants would say it is also the price of a duopoly, and they are not entirely wrong.

But the fat issuer margin is also the engine. Two percent on every swipe is what funds the cashback, the airline miles, and the "0% for 10 months" installment plans Thai banks advertise at every mall. Rewards pull in cardholders. Cardholders pull in merchants. More merchants make the card more useful, which pulls in more cardholders. The network takes a sliver of an ever larger pie.

Why the noodle shop says PromptPay only

Now put the same ฿1,000 through PromptPay. The Bank of Thailand priced the rail so a consumer transfer is free and a merchant QR payment costs the shop nothing or close to it. There is no interchange, so there is no rewards engine, but there is also no ฿25 leaving the till. For a small shop running on thin margins, that decides it.

The Bank of Thailand also capped debit card fees for merchants at 0.55%, which is why your debit card is cheap for the shop and your credit card is not. And it is why the "credit card +3%" sign you see at markets exists. Passing the fee on breaks the network's rules, but enforcement against a stall in Chatuchak is not a priority for anyone.

The European Union got the same result by law: interchange is capped at 0.3%. Thailand got it by building a public rival. Either way, once a merchant has a near-free rail, card rewards get thinner and acceptance gets pickier.

STAGE 4 · when it goes wrongDispute

Suppose the bike never gets fixed and the shop stops answering LINE. With cash or PromptPay, your remedy is the Office of the Consumer Protection Board or small claims court, and the money is already gone. With a card, you call KBank. The fourth job of the network is to write the rules that make that call work.

Visa's rulebook runs to 923 pages and is public. It covers how the logo may be printed, what data a transaction must carry, how fast each party must respond, and, most importantly, who is liable when. Banks that break the rules face fines from $25,000 to $1,000,000 a month, or expulsion.

Notice that the network barely appears. A dispute is forced arbitration between KBank and the shop's acquirer. They trade evidence until one side gives up and accepts the loss. Visa only steps in if neither yields, and it charges $600 to look, $1,000 for an appeal, paid by the loser on top of the disputed amount. For a ฿1,000 repair, that referee fee is about twenty times the bill.

Those fees are the design. Nobody wants to reach the referee. Issuers usually refund the customer and write off small amounts. Merchants refund unhappy customers on their own, because their acquirer bills them a dispute fee the moment a chargeback is filed, win or lose. The rules do not so much resolve disputes as make disputes expensive enough that everyone resolves them first.

It is not a fair system. It is a fast one. It also leans toward the cardholder, which is what makes a tourist comfortable handing a card to a stranger in a country whose courts they will never use. That protection is the one thing PromptPay does not give you. A QR transfer is final the moment you press confirm.

What you would need to build one

Strip the brand away and a card network is four things. Thailand actually built one, so the checklist has a local column.

The first is engineering. The second is a balance sheet. The third and fourth are choices. Thailand chose cheap and final. Visa chose expensive and reversible. Both are networks. The receipt just tells you which one you rode.
